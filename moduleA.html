What is Projectile Motion?

Projectile motion is a form of motion experienced by an object that is projected near the Earth's surface and moves along a curved path under the action of gravity only. The path followed by a projectile is called its trajectory, which is a parabola.

The key insight is that projectile motion can be analyzed as two independent motions: horizontal motion with constant velocity, and vertical motion with constant acceleration (due to gravity).

➡️

Horizontal Motion

No acceleration (ignoring air resistance). Velocity remains constant throughout the flight.

⬇️

Vertical Motion

Constant acceleration due to gravity (g ≈ 9.8 m/s²). Velocity changes continuously.

🎯

Independence

Horizontal and vertical motions are completely independent of each other.

Assumptions in Ideal Projectile Motion

The standard model of projectile motion makes several simplifying assumptions:

  • Air resistance is negligible
  • Gravitational acceleration is constant (flat Earth approximation)
  • The curvature of Earth is negligible for the distances involved
  • The projectile is a point mass (rotation effects ignored)

Mathematical Equations

Velocity Components

The initial velocity is decomposed into horizontal and vertical components:

v₀ₓ = v₀ · cos(θ)
Horizontal component of initial velocity
v₀ᵧ = v₀ · sin(θ)
Vertical component of initial velocity

Position Equations

The position of the projectile at any time t:

x(t) = v₀ₓ · t = v₀ · cos(θ) · t
Horizontal position (uniform motion)
y(t) = h₀ + v₀ᵧ · t - ½gt² = h₀ + v₀ · sin(θ) · t - ½gt²
Vertical position (uniformly accelerated motion)

Velocity at Time t

vₓ(t) = v₀ₓ = v₀ · cos(θ)
Horizontal velocity remains constant
vᵧ(t) = v₀ᵧ - gt = v₀ · sin(θ) - gt
Vertical velocity decreases, then becomes negative
v(t) = √(vₓ² + vᵧ²)
Total velocity magnitude at any time

Trajectory Equation

Eliminating time from the position equations gives the path:

y = h₀ + x · tan(θ) - (g · x²) / (2 · v₀² · cos²(θ))
Parabolic trajectory equation (y as a function of x)

Key Derived Quantities

T = (v₀ · sin(θ) + √((v₀ · sin(θ))² + 2gh₀)) / g
Time of flight (for launch height h₀)
T = (2 · v₀ · sin(θ)) / g
Time of flight (when h₀ = 0, landing at same height)
H = h₀ + (v₀² · sin²(θ)) / (2g)
Maximum height reached
R = v₀ · cos(θ) · T
Range (horizontal distance)
R = (v₀² · sin(2θ)) / g
Range when h₀ = 0 (maximum at θ = 45°)

🌍

The 45° Myth — and Why Reality Differs

While 45° maximises range on flat ground in a vacuum, real athletes rarely use it. Long jumpers prefer ~20° to preserve sprinting speed; javelin throwers use ~35° due to air resistance; basketball players prefer >50° for a better entry angle. Physics is just the starting point — optimal technique depends on the full system.
